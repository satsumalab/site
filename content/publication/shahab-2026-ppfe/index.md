title: "Association of progressive pleuroparenchymal fibroelastosis and mortality in lung cancer screening cohorts: a retrospective study"
subtitle: ''
summary: "Pleuroparenchymal fibroelastosis (PPFE) is a type of scarring that mainly affects the top of the lungs. Using repeat CT scans from over 16,500 people in two lung cancer screening studies, the US National Lung Screening Trial and the London-based SUMMIT study, we used an automated deep-learning tool to measure how much PPFE each person had and how quickly it grew. People whose PPFE grew over time were more likely to die during follow-up, and in SUMMIT they were also admitted to hospital more often for lung problems and needed more steroids or antibiotics for chest symptoms. Because these scans are already taken for screening, tracking PPFE could help identify people who need closer respiratory follow-up."

abstract: "Background: Pleuroparenchymal fibroelastosis (PPFE) is an upper lobe-predominant fibrotic lung abnormality associated with increased mortality in established interstitial lung disease. However, the clinical significance of radiologic PPFE progression in lung cancer screening (LCS) populations remains unclear. In this study, we aimed to evaluate whether radiological PPFE progression was independently associated with all-cause mortality in two lung cancer screening cohorts. Methods: We analysed longitudinal low-dose CT scans and clinical and mortality follow-up data from two LCS cohorts comprising current or former smokers at high risk of lung cancer: the US National Lung Screening Trial (NLST; n=7980; aged 55–74 years at enrolment), with screening conducted between August, 2002, and September, 2007, and the London-based SUMMIT study (n=8561; aged 55–77 years at enrolment), conducted between April, 2019, and May, 2023. Participants included in this analysis had baseline and follow-up CT scans and available mortality follow-up data. An automated algorithm quantified PPFE volume in the baseline and follow-up scans. Annualised change in PPFE was derived and dichotomised using a distribution-based threshold to define progressive PPFE. The primary endpoint was all-cause mortality. Associations between progressive PPFE and mortality were evaluated for both LCS cohorts using Cox proportional hazards models adjusted for demographic and clinical variables. In the SUMMIT cohort, associations between progressive PPFE and clinical outcomes were assessed in terms of incidence rate ratios (IRRs) and odds ratios (ORs). Findings: Progressive PPFE was identified in 431 of 7980 participants (5·4%) in NLST and 124 of 8561 (1·5%) in SUMMIT. Among these participants, mean PPFE volume increased from 0·70 cm³ to 1·38 cm³ in NLST and from 2·39 cm³ to 3·83 cm³ in SUMMIT, with mean annualised increases of 0·69 cm³/year (SD 0·37) and 0·65 cm³/year (SD 0·24), respectively. Progressive PPFE was independently associated with mortality in both LCS cohorts (NLST: hazard ratio 1·25, 95% CI 1·01–1·56, p=0·042; SUMMIT: 3·14, 1·66–5·97, p=0·0004). Within SUMMIT, progressive PPFE was strongly associated with higher respiratory admissions (IRR 2·79, p<0·0001), increased steroid or antibiotic use or both (1·55, p=0·011), and showed an imprecise association with modified Medical Research Council dyspnoea score that did not reach significance (OR 1·40, p=0·055). Interpretation: Radiologic PPFE progression was independently associated with mortality across the two large LCS cohorts and also associated with adverse clinical outcomes in the SUMMIT cohort. Quantitative assessment of PPFE progression could provide a clinically relevant imaging biomarker to identify individuals at increased risk of respiratory morbidity within LCS programmes.
